I set out to make one video explaining how I manage projects. A tutorial. Maybe a weekend.

What it actually required: **a microphone, a camera, lighting, OBS, an audio editor, the Adobe suite, a communication angle, and a thumbnail** — the last of which turned out to need Photoshop skills I did not have.

That's six jobs stacked into one deliverable, and nobody tells you that when you decide to "start making videos". Here is the stack I ended up with and, more usefully, the order I'd learn it in if I started again.

## The six jobs hiding inside one video {#six-jobs}

Written out honestly, a single tutorial video is:

1. **Writing** — deciding what it's about and what to cut
2. **Sound engineering** — mic, room, levels, cleanup
3. **Lighting and camera** — the part that looks like the whole job and isn't
4. **Editing** — pacing, cuts, structure
5. **Colour and finishing** — grading, consistency across shots
6. **Packaging** — title, thumbnail, the promise the video makes

Every one of those is a discipline people spend careers on. As a solo dev you're not going to be good at six things. **You're going to be adequate at five and deliberately good at the one that carries the video.**

## Audio first, always {#audio-first}

If you take one thing from this: **people forgive a bad picture and leave over bad sound.**

A slightly soft, slightly dark image reads as "homemade" and viewers stay. Room echo, clipping, or an inconsistent level reads as *unwatchable*, and the tab closes before your first point lands.

So the first real purchase is a microphone, and the first real skill is the room — soft surfaces, distance from walls, and recording at a level that isn't fighting for its life in post. Everything you fix at the source is time you don't spend in an audio editor later, and time in an audio editor is the least enjoyable time in this whole process.

## Capture: OBS and a room you control {#capture}

**OBS** does the recording, and it's free, which makes the build-versus-buy question easy.

The thing worth setting up properly once: scenes. Screen capture, camera, and the combination of both, each as its own scene, so switching between "look at my face" and "look at this Notion database" is one key rather than an editing decision made three days later.

Lighting matters less than people think and more than nothing. One controlled source beats three uncontrolled ones. The real goal is **consistency between takes** — a shot that changes brightness mid-video is far more distracting than a shot that's dim throughout.

---

## Editing is where the pace is decided {#editing}

This is the job I underestimated most.

The raw recording of anything technical is slow, because the person recording it knows the subject and is being careful. **Editing is the act of removing the carefulness.** Dynamic cuts, no dead air, and sound design carrying the transitions — that's the difference between a video someone finishes and one they abandon at ninety seconds.

Sound design in particular does disproportionate work. A cut with a small sound on it reads as intentional; the same cut silent reads as a mistake. It's the same principle as game feel — the [haptics and audio on our weapon system]({{ '/2026/06/28/VR-WEAPON-SYSTEM/' | relative_url }}) do exactly this job for exactly the same reason.

## The thumbnail is not the last step {#thumbnail}

Here's the mistake I made for a long time: treating the thumbnail as packaging you slap on at the end.

The thumbnail and title are **the promise**. They decide whether the six other jobs get seen at all. And they're a genuine craft — I had to learn Photoshop properly to do them: **skin tones**, which are the hardest thing to get non-awful, and **dynamic lighting** on the subject so the image reads at 200 pixels wide.

## Long form and shorts are different crafts {#shorts}

I make both, and treating them as the same job with different runtimes was wrong.

**Long form** can afford context. You can explain why something matters before showing it, and the viewer has already decided to invest.

**Shorts** cannot. There's no setup budget at all — the first second either earns the second second or nothing else in the video exists. The skill isn't compression, it's **choosing something that doesn't need a setup.**

Practically, that means shorts are not offcuts from long videos. A short built from a highlight of a tutorial almost always fails, because the highlight was only meaningful given the two minutes before it.

## The order I'd learn it in {#order}

If I were starting over, with limited time:

1. **Audio.** Cheapest to fix, highest cost when wrong.
2. **Writing and cutting.** A well-structured video shot on a phone beats a beautiful video with no spine.
3. **Editing pace and sound design.** The largest gain per hour learned.
4. **Thumbnails and titles.** Learn this before you think you're ready, because it gates everything upstream.
5. **Lighting and camera.** Genuinely last. It's the most visible and the least decisive.
6. **Colour.** A finishing skill; useful, never the reason a video worked.

The general shape of that list: **the things viewers consciously notice are near the bottom.**

## FAQ {#faq}

<details class="ds-accordion__item">
<summary class="ds-accordion__trigger"><span>Do I need the Adobe suite?</span><span class="ds-accordion__icon" aria-hidden="true"></span></summary>
<div class="ds-accordion__panel" markdown="1">
No. It's what I use, and there are free alternatives for every part of it that are good enough that tooling isn't your bottleneck — your bottleneck is the writing and the pacing. Buy software when a specific limitation is costing you time, not before.
</div>
</details>

<details class="ds-accordion__item">
<summary class="ds-accordion__trigger"><span>How long does one video actually take?</span><span class="ds-accordion__icon" aria-hidden="true"></span></summary>
<div class="ds-accordion__panel" markdown="1">
Much longer than the recording, and the ratio surprises everyone at first. The recording is a small fraction; writing, editing, and packaging are the rest. Plan for it as a production task with a real cost, not as something you fit around development, or it will quietly consume a sprint.
</div>
</details>

<details class="ds-accordion__item">
<summary class="ds-accordion__trigger"><span>Is video worth it for a game studio at all?</span><span class="ds-accordion__icon" aria-hidden="true"></span></summary>
<div class="ds-accordion__panel" markdown="1">
It's the format that consistently comes out on top when I discuss this with other founders — for a visual product, showing beats describing. What matters more than the format is the consistency, which is the part most of us fail rather than the production quality.
</div>
</details>

<details class="ds-accordion__item">
<summary class="ds-accordion__trigger"><span>How do you A/B test a thumbnail?</span><span class="ds-accordion__icon" aria-hidden="true"></span></summary>
<div class="ds-accordion__panel" markdown="1">
Make genuinely different versions rather than three variations of one idea — different composition, different promise — and let the platform's testing decide. The value isn't the small percentage you win; it's discovering that your instinct about what appeals was wrong, which then improves everything after it.
</div>
</details>
