I've spent two years doing industry events: a large XR trade show, a studio gathering, local afterworks, and sessions where you hand your build to other developers.

The pattern is sharper than I expected: **an event's value is roughly inversely proportional to the number of talks I attended.**

Here's what each format actually returns, and which one I'd keep if I could only do one.

## The big trade show: seeing what exists {#trade-show}

**Laval Virtual** is Europe's largest XR event, and I went for the first time with a simple question: what's actually being built, outside of what crosses my feed?

The answer was interesting. A lot of **phygital** — experiences combining VR with real physical controllers, objects you genuinely manipulate. And a conference programme whose content surprised me: training, design, testing, marketing, commerce. **Games are a minority of that room.**

<img src="https://mcusercontent.com/ba845a58e9756f53027feeec5/images/2d9080d4-9b60-43d8-6070-f9979ba4bf80.gif" alt="XR experiences at Laval Virtual" class="medium"/>

That's what a big trade show does best: **correct your map of the sector.** If your idea of VR comes from what you follow, it's distorted by your own feed. A day at a show shows you the professional uses actually funding the technology you build on.

What it does worst: meeting people. Too many, too much flow, too many there to sell.

## The gathering: stop going to talks {#one-to-one}

At **ADDON** I did the opposite of my first time out. Almost no talks. One-to-one meetings with other developers, all day.

It was incomparably more productive, and it isn't mysterious. **A talk is content, and content is available.** The recording will come out, or the equivalent already exists somewhere. What isn't available anywhere else is thirty minutes with someone who has your problem and a different solution to it.

<img src="https://mcusercontent.com/ba845a58e9756f53027feeec5/images/2e60d0da-1702-095f-ba25-5ea012d8b063.png" alt="Developer meetings at ADDON" class="small"/>
<img src="https://mcusercontent.com/ba845a58e9756f53027feeec5/images/10e0918e-419b-c3db-fcd3-89c5a436ea21.png" alt="Exchanging best practices during the event" class="small"/>

The rule I take from it: **go to talks when you don't know what to ask.** The moment you have a real question, go and find the person, not the room.

## The most useful format: getting playtested {#playtest}

**Builds de Neige** is a gathering where studios play each other's projects and trade feedback. Organised by the Atlangames association, hosted by a local studio.

It is by a distance the most useful format on this list.

<img src="https://mcusercontent.com/ba845a58e9756f53027feeec5/images/f7fd4dad-2b2a-28d7-63d6-08d9d56ec4d9.png" alt="Studios playtesting each other's projects at Builds de Neige" class="medium"/>

The reason is simple: **it's the only place you get feedback from people who know what they're looking at.** A player tells you something is wrong. Another developer tells you *why*, and often what fixing it would cost. That level of feedback can't be bought and can't be requested over a message.

The cost is that you have to bring a build. A real one, playable by a stranger, without you standing behind their shoulder explaining. That constraint alone justifies signing up: **it forces you to have a presentable version on a fixed date.**

## The local meetup: the hidden return {#local}

The format I most underestimated at the start. An evening, a technical talk, and people.

<img src="https://mcusercontent.com/ba845a58e9756f53027feeec5/images/fcf0b12d-025b-646c-4c57-ce19bda09a35.jpg" alt="A game dev afterwork organised by Atlangames" class="small"/>
<img src="https://mcusercontent.com/ba845a58e9756f53027feeec5/images/d7d5e993-b50c-b070-e947-bd6ae0902166.gif" alt="Workshops and games at an Atlangames seminar" class="small"/>

One of those evenings covered the Phaser engine, with a presentation where the code executed directly inside the slides — the kind of detail you remember because it's well made. But that isn't the reason to go.

**You go for the recurrence.** The same faces come back, and after a few months you actually know people rather than having exchanged cards. Most of the collaborations that matter to me now came out of those evenings — including the [monthly office hours]({{ '/2026/07/13/OFFICE-HOURS-FOUNDERS/' | relative_url }}) that remain my best return on time.

A big show gives you a hundred contacts. A local meetup gives you three, and you'll see them again.

## Crossing over: presenting {#speaking}

At some point I was asked to speak about organisation in front of a developer community. I said yes mostly because declining was more uncomfortable than going.

Two things I learned:

**Preparing a talk forces you to know what you think.** Same effect as [assembling a grant application]({{ '/2026/07/01/CNC-GRANT-INDIE-STUDIO/' | relative_url }}) — the formulation exercise is worth it independently of the audience.

**The questions are the deliverable.** What people ask afterwards tells you what they don't understand about what you do, which is exactly the information the rest of your communication can never get at.

## What about publishers? {#publishers}

It's the stated reason a lot of studios do shows, so it's worth being honest about.

We prepared a Gamescom run exactly the way you're meant to: reworked pitch, trailer, GIFs, sharpened promotional material, aiming at VR-focused publishers.

<img src="https://mcusercontent.com/ba845a58e9756f53027feeec5/images/b2c66c15-a2de-cfe8-aa41-7e0680cea22a.png" alt="Preparing the pitch and materials for Gamescom" class="small"/>
<img src="https://mcusercontent.com/ba845a58e9756f53027feeec5/images/dc43da4a-6f36-973b-92a9-dfae22b1b2f7.png" alt="Promotional materials prepared for the show" class="small"/>

What I take from it: **the preparation is the part you own.** A sharpened pitch, a trailer that holds, visuals that work out of context — you keep all of it, and it feeds the Steam page, the funding application, the newsletter. The publisher meeting depends on their current slate, their cash position and their calendar, which is to say on things you don't control.

Go for the forced preparation. Treat the meetings as upside.

## If I could only keep one {#one-only}

The studio playtest session, without hesitation.

It's the only format that produces both **expert feedback on the game** and **a real deadline**, while putting you in a room with people whose week looks like yours. A big trade show costs more, takes longer, and sends you home with impressions rather than with fixes.

After that, in order: the local meetup, the one-to-one gathering, and the big show last — once every couple of years, to recalibrate the map.

## FAQ {#faq}

<div class="ds-accordion ds-accordion--flush">

<details class="ds-accordion__item">
<summary class="ds-accordion__trigger"><span>Is it worth going with nothing to show?</span><span class="ds-accordion__icon" aria-hidden="true"></span></summary>
<div class="ds-accordion__panel" markdown="1">
Meetups and gatherings, yes — turning up to listen and ask questions is entirely legitimate, and nobody checks your catalogue. Playtest sessions, no: the format depends on bringing something, and arriving empty-handed means missing the thing that makes it valuable.
</div>
</details>

<details class="ds-accordion__item">
<summary class="ds-accordion__trigger"><span>How do you approach people if you hate doing it?</span><span class="ds-accordion__icon" aria-hidden="true"></span></summary>
<div class="ds-accordion__panel" markdown="1">
Arrive with a specific technical question rather than an introduction. "How are you handling locomotion comfort?" starts a conversation; "I make VR games" starts nothing. The question moves the weight of the exchange onto the subject, which is much easier on everyone.
</div>
</details>

<details class="ds-accordion__item">
<summary class="ds-accordion__trigger"><span>How many events a year is reasonable?</span><span class="ds-accordion__icon" aria-hidden="true"></span></summary>
<div class="ds-accordion__panel" markdown="1">
Few big ones, many small ones. A large show costs several days across travel, attendance and recovery; a local evening costs an evening. Return per hour tilts heavily toward the local and the recurring, which is the opposite of what you assume starting out.
</div>
</details>

<details class="ds-accordion__item">
<summary class="ds-accordion__trigger"><span>Do you need a booth?</span><span class="ds-accordion__icon" aria-hidden="true"></span></summary>
<div class="ds-accordion__panel" markdown="1">
Not to begin with. A booth pins you in place while a show's value is in moving around and in scheduled meetings. Consider one when you have something to put in people's hands and enough bodies to staff it without losing your own day to it.
</div>
</details>
