In January 2024 we submitted a **conception grant** application to the CNC — France's national film and moving-image body, which also funds video games — for *Vermin Exterminator*. We were told to expect an answer within two months.

What nobody had told me is that **the application is useful before anyone reads it.** You fill it in for the money. What you get back is a project that can finally describe itself.

Here is what the exercise asks for, and what it exposes.

## Why an indie studio goes after this at all {#why}

The reasoning is simple. A VR game takes months during which it earns nothing. Someone has to fund those months: you, a publisher, contract work, or public money.

Public money has one advantage that isn't the money: **it doesn't take anything from you.** No equity, no editorial control, no commercial deadline imposed from outside. You still own what you build.

It also has a real cost: **the time to assemble the file**, and the uncertainty. You write for days without knowing whether it lands. That's the calculation to make honestly before you start.

## What the application actually asks {#the-form}

Ours described, section by section: **the origin of the project, its references, the universe, the story, the visual style, the gameplay mechanics.**

Written out like that it looks like a form. In practice each section is a question we had never had to answer properly.

**Origin.** Why this game, why you, why now. Easy to tell over a drink, genuinely hard to write as one page that stands up in front of someone who doesn't know you.

**Universe and story.** A VR game can go a long way with no lore document at all. The application doesn't allow that. You have to write down what until then existed as a tacit agreement between the people on the team.

**Visual style.** Not "what we like", but **what makes a screenshot of this game recognisable**. That's where you find out whether your art direction is a decision or an accumulation.

**Mechanics.** The part a developer feels at home in — which is the trap. You start listing features when the question being asked is: *what does it feel like to play?*

---

## The question that hurts: your references {#references}

If I had to name the section that teaches the most, it's this one.

Naming your references means **placing yourself**. You can no longer say "it's kind of its own thing". You have to write down the titles you're standing next to, and at that moment one of two things happens:

- the references are coherent, and the project has an identifiable place in the market;
- or they aren't, and you have just discovered that the project hasn't decided what it is yet.

The second case isn't bad news. It is **far cheaper to discover in a document than six months later in production.**

## The side effect: you just wrote your pitch {#side-effect}

Once the file was sent, we realised we were holding something else.

The origin text becomes the first slide of a pitch deck. The universe section feeds the Steam page. The visual style gives you the brief to send an artist. The mechanics give you the script for a trailer — we had just released a new one, with story elements and new locations, and the consistency between the two was not a coincidence.

<img src="https://mcusercontent.com/ba845a58e9756f53027feeec5/images/012251b1-729a-e173-896b-2396a01cf111.png" alt="The new cinematic trailer for Vermin Exterminator" class="medium"/>

**A grant application is a formulation exercise disguised as paperwork.** Even rejected, it leaves you the text.

## What I would do differently {#differently}

Three things, with hindsight.

**Start with the visuals, not the text.** A universe page written before you have images is a page you rewrite afterwards.

**Don't wait for an application to write those sections.** They should have existed from the start, kept current as the project moved. The application would then have been a formatting job instead of a writing sprint.

**Budget the admin time as real production time.** It isn't time stolen from the project; it's time invested in its clarity. But it has to go in the schedule, or it gets paid for out of your nights.

---

## What it does not replace {#not-a-plan}

A conception grant funds a phase, not a studio.

Our months are actually funded by a mix: **contract work, grants, and the product we sell alongside.** Each brick has a flaw — contract work eats production time, grants are slow and uncertain, a product needs maintaining. The mix holds.

The advice I'd give a studio starting out: **apply, but don't build your schedule on the answer.** A project whose survival depends on a committee is a project holding its breath.

> ⚠️ The names, criteria and calendars of CNC schemes shift from year to year. What I describe is the experience of one file submitted in early 2024 — check the current terms on the CNC site before you start. If you're outside France, the equivalent bodies differ but the questions on the form are strikingly similar.

## FAQ {#faq}

<summary class="ds-accordion__trigger"><span>Do you need a playable game to apply?</span><span class="ds-accordion__icon" aria-hidden="true"></span></summary>
<div class="ds-accordion__panel" markdown="1">
A conception grant targets exactly the phase where the game isn't there yet. But you need enough to make the project credible: visuals, a clear intent, and preferably something that moves. We already had a game in development, which helps — a purely textual file asks a great deal more of the reader.

</details>

<details class="ds-accordion__item">
<summary class="ds-accordion__trigger"><span>How long does it take to put together?</span><span class="ds-accordion__icon" aria-hidden="true"></span></summary>
<div class="ds-accordion__panel" markdown="1">
Much longer than expected, for a specific reason: it isn't filling in, it's deciding. The time doesn't go into the writing but into the calls the writing forces. Budget generously, and treat that time as serving the project even if the application fails.
</div>
</details>

<details class="ds-accordion__item">
<summary class="ds-accordion__trigger"><span>Is it worth it for a solo developer?</span><span class="ds-accordion__icon" aria-hidden="true"></span></summary>
<div class="ds-accordion__panel" markdown="1">
The exercise, yes — the writing is the same whatever the team size. The economics, check for yourself: the smaller the team, the heavier the assembly time weighs against everything else. Look at regional schemes too, which usually have less competition.
</div>
</details>

<details class="ds-accordion__item">
<summary class="ds-accordion__trigger"><span>What if it's refused?</span><span class="ds-accordion__icon" aria-hidden="true"></span></summary>
<div class="ds-accordion__panel" markdown="1">
You keep the text, the visuals and the decisions. That's the real reason to apply even when you rate your odds as low: the secondary deliverable is yours either way.
