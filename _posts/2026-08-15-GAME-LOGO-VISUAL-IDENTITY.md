A game logo isn't a company logo. A company logo has to be memorable over years. **A game logo has about a fifth of a second, at 200 pixels wide, next to a thousand others, to answer one question: what kind of game is this?**

When we redid the identity for *Vermin Exterminator*, that reframing is what made the work tractable. We stopped asking "what looks good" and started asking "what does someone conclude from this in a store grid".

Here's the process, and the part that turned out to matter most.

## The actual job of a game logo {#the-job}

Three constraints that make this different from ordinary branding:

**It is seen at thumbnail size first.** Store grids, capsule images, social previews. If it only works at full size, it doesn't work.

**It's seen next to competitors.** Nobody encounters your logo in isolation. It's in a row, and the question it has to win is not "is this nice" but "is this the kind of thing I want right now".

**It has to declare a genre and a tone.** A player scanning a store is sorting, not appreciating. Your logo's real function is to get sorted into the right pile.

That third one is why the design brief can't start with taste. **It has to start with what you want someone to conclude.**

## The mood board came before any drawing {#mood-board}

Before a single logo sketch, we built a mood board. It felt like a delay. It was the most useful step in the whole process.

<img src="https://mcusercontent.com/ba845a58e9756f53027feeec5/images/c933bd84-67b9-4376-04f8-8efa91c6df20.png" alt="The visual identity mood board for Vermin Exterminator" class="medium"/>

The mood board's value isn't inspiration. It's that it **turns taste into a decision that can be argued about.** "I don't like it" is unanswerable. "That's outside the palette we agreed" is a conversation with an end.

It also survives the logo. Everything downstream — the store page, the trailer, the merch, the [thumbnails]({{ '/2026/07/28/SOLO-VIDEO-STACK/' | relative_url }}) — refers back to it. You do the thinking once.

---

## Four colours, each doing a job {#four-colours}

The palette we landed on wasn't chosen for looking good together. Each entry was carrying meaning:

- **Radioactive green** — the toxic, chemical side of pest control. The colour that says this isn't realistic.
- **Pest greys and browns** — the vermin themselves, and the grubbiness of the spaces they live in.
- **Metallic sheen** — the technology. The gauntlet, the equipment, the part of the game that's science fiction rather than horror.
- **Warning-sign yellows and reds** — danger and industrial signage. The visual language of "hazard" that everyone already reads without being taught.

That last one is worth dwelling on. **Borrowing an existing visual language is free comprehension.** Nobody has to learn what hazard yellow means. If your game is about dealing with something dangerous, you're allowed to use the colours the real world already uses for that.

The tension in the palette — toxic green against clean metal — is also the tension in the game: technology versus poison. When the palette encodes the premise, everything drawn from it is on-message without anyone trying.

## Choosing the image: a rat attacking a gauntlet {#the-image}

The concept we committed to: **a rat attacking the player's gauntlet.**

Not a rat. Not a gauntlet. The two of them in conflict — because that image contains a verb. A rat alone says "there are rats in this game". A rat attacking your equipment says **this is a game where you fight them, with tools, and they fight back.**

<img src="https://mcusercontent.com/ba845a58e9756f53027feeec5/images/c082bbdc-b5f2-66e3-f46e-31bb117e4bab.png" alt="Work in progress on the game logo" class="medium"/>

That's the test I'd apply to any game logo: **does it contain an action, or just a subject?** Subjects tell people what's in the game. Actions tell them what they'll be doing, which is the thing they're actually deciding about.

## The revision that fixed it {#menacing}

The first pass was fine and slightly wrong, in a way that took an outside eye to name: the rat wasn't threatening enough, and the gauntlet didn't read as advanced technology.

The final version — drawn by Pauline, who also did the UI work on our [immersive learning module]({{ '/2026/07/07/VR-TRAINING-DESIGN/' | relative_url }}) — pushed both: **a more menacing creature, and a visibly high-tech gauntlet.**

<img src="https://mcusercontent.com/ba845a58e9756f53027feeec5/images/d84d4d31-7aa9-d7c4-96bd-00008aa01151.png" alt="The final Vermin Exterminator logo" class="medium"/>

Both changes did the same thing: they raised the stakes of the image. A cute rat and a plain glove describe a chore. A menacing rat and a serious piece of equipment describe a fight. **Same composition, completely different genre signal.**

This is the argument for working with someone who does this professionally. I could see that the first version wasn't landing. I could not have told you it was a stakes problem.

## A logo is only useful if it survives everywhere {#everywhere}

The identity work doesn't end at the logo file. It ends when every surface agrees.

**The store page.** We rebuilt ours around a green, smoky aesthetic — the same technology-versus-poison balance, held across a whole page rather than one image. A store page that contradicts the logo makes the visitor do reconciliation work at exactly the moment you want them deciding.

**Merchandise.** We refreshed the store with the game and studio logos, which is the least glamorous test and one of the most revealing: **a logo that doesn't survive being printed on fabric at one colour was never as simple as you thought.**

<img src="https://mcusercontent.com/ba845a58e9756f53027feeec5/images/a2eadcaa-545e-27ac-661d-28534cc7bcca.gif" alt="Refreshed merchandise carrying the game and studio logos" class="medium"/>

**The game itself.** The palette has to hold up under the game's own lighting, which is the constraint nobody plans for. A colour chosen in a design tool can behave very differently in an engine.

---

## What I'd do differently {#lessons}

**Do the mood board even earlier.** It's the cheapest artefact in the process and it makes every later argument shorter. We treated it as the start of the logo work; it should have been the start of the project's art direction.

**Test at thumbnail size from the first sketch.** Not at the end. A logo that has to be shrunk to be judged is being judged in the wrong order.

**Write down what the image should make someone conclude.** One sentence. It's what lets you tell "I don't like it" apart from "it's saying the wrong thing", and only the second one is actionable.

**Bring in someone who does this.** The genre signal is subtle, and the difference between a version that works and one that nearly works is not something you can reliably see in your own project.

## FAQ {#faq}

<div class="ds-accordion ds-accordion--flush">

<details class="ds-accordion__item">
<summary class="ds-accordion__trigger"><span>When should a game get its logo?</span><span class="ds-accordion__icon" aria-hidden="true"></span></summary>
<div class="ds-accordion__panel" markdown="1">
Once you know its genre and tone, and before you need a store page, a pitch deck or a trailer. Too early and you're designing for a game that might change; too late and you'll have shipped half a dozen assets that don't match each other and will need redoing.
</div>
</details>

<details class="ds-accordion__item">
<summary class="ds-accordion__trigger"><span>Can you do this yourself as a solo dev?</span><span class="ds-accordion__icon" aria-hidden="true"></span></summary>
<div class="ds-accordion__panel" markdown="1">
The mood board and the brief, absolutely — those are decisions about your own game, and nobody is better placed. The execution is where an outside professional earns their fee, especially on the tone calibration that separates a version that lands from one that nearly does.
</div>
</details>

<details class="ds-accordion__item">
<summary class="ds-accordion__trigger"><span>Should the studio logo and the game logo match?</span><span class="ds-accordion__icon" aria-hidden="true"></span></summary>
<div class="ds-accordion__panel" markdown="1">
They should be compatible, not identical. They're doing different jobs — the studio mark carries across projects with different tones, while the game mark has to commit hard to one genre. Sharing construction principles while differing in palette usually gets you both.
</div>
</details>

<details class="ds-accordion__item">
<summary class="ds-accordion__trigger"><span>How many colours should a palette have?</span><span class="ds-accordion__icon" aria-hidden="true"></span></summary>
<div class="ds-accordion__panel" markdown="1">
Few enough that each one has a job you can name. Ours was four, and being able to say what each was for is what made it usable — a palette you can't explain is a collection of colours you happen to like, and it won't survive its first disagreement.
</div>
</details>
