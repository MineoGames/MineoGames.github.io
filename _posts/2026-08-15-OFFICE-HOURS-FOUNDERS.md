Since late 2023 I've been meeting regularly with the founders of two other companies — **La Flamboyante** and **Stablemind** — for what we call *office hours*. Not a networking group, not an event. A meeting where we tell each other plainly what isn't working.

Hour for hour, it's the highest-return investment I've made since the studio existed. And almost never for the reason you'd expect.

## The format, in one sentence {#format}

Founders from adjacent but non-competing fields meet on a fixed schedule, each says where they actually are, and the others respond without softening it.

Three details carry everything else:

**Non-competing.** A communications agency, a design studio, a game studio. Nobody loses when someone else wins, so nobody has a reason to hold back.

**Recurring.** A one-off meeting produces congratulations. A recurring one produces follow-up — *"what happened with the thing you mentioned last month?"* is the most useful question in the room.

**Nothing to sell.** The moment there's a transaction involved, feedback goes polite. The fact that no money moves is what keeps it honest.

## Value number one: unpolished feedback {#feedback}

What I expected: advice. What I got: **questions I couldn't answer.**

Nobody in this group makes video games. That's exactly what makes their questions dangerous. Another developer immediately understands why a given technical decision was reasonable. Someone from communications asks *who is this for*, and you have to actually answer.

The feedback isn't always pleasant. That's the point. **Pleasant feedback is feedback that changed nothing.**

## The five fundamentals we keep repeating {#fundamentals}

Across two mentoring sessions in particular, we ended up isolating five things everything else reduces to. They're embarrassingly basic, which is precisely why they keep coming back:

1. **Set a budget.** Not an estimate. A written number that exists before the spending does.
2. **Track KPIs.** Few of them, but genuinely looked at. A metric nobody opens is decoration.
3. **Know your audience.** Specifically who. "VR players" is not an audience, it's an industry.
4. **Hold operational rigour.** What was decided gets done, or gets re-decided — but explicitly.
5. **Execute the plan, over time.** The hardest of the five by a distance.

None of these is a revelation. All five are things you stop doing the moment production takes over again, which is exactly why you need someone asking where you're at.

## The seminar that changed how we sell {#soncas}

The format eventually outgrew the monthly meeting. Over two days, with the same companies, we worked on **brand strategy** and the **SONCAS** method.

SONCAS is a French sales framework listing buyer motivations — security, pride, novelty, comfort, money, and rapport. It sounds like something out of a sales manual, and it is. Its usefulness, for someone with a technical background, is that it **replaces intuition with a checklist**: you stop selling the motivations that appeal to *you* and start looking at which ones apply to the person in front of you.

Concretely, those two days had us identify **secondary markets** we hadn't considered. The same capability, aimed at someone else. That's the kind of conclusion that never comes out of thinking alone, because you don't ask yourself questions you believe you already know the answer to.

## Consistency beats brilliance {#consistency}

One early-year session was about communication. After an hour of discussion, the consensus across all three companies came down to two words: **post consistently, authentically** — with video as the format that works best.

It isn't spectacular. It never made a headline. But it's the conclusion three businesses in different sectors reached independently, and **it's exactly the kind of finding you won't allow yourself alone**, because it looks too simple to be the answer.

Other sessions covered frameworks brought in from outside — Nathan Barry's ladder of wealth, which I presented to the group and still consider the best tool I've found for understanding the different ways a business can be run; sales and conversion-rate work the others brought. Every framework is arguable. Having to explain one to three people who will go and test it, much less so.

## What it really fixes: isolation {#isolation}

Here's the part I hadn't anticipated.

Running a small studio means making every decision without ever knowing whether it's normal. Was this a bad month, or is it like this for everyone? Is this doubt a signal, or is it Tuesday?

**A founder group answers that question**, and no amount of advice substitutes for it. Entrepreneurship in the creative industries isn't a solo sport — it just looks like one, because the visible part of the work happens alone in front of a screen.

## How to start your own {#build-yours}

If you want to reproduce this, four decisions do it:

**Three companies, not ten.** Beyond that it becomes a conference and people stop saying the awkward things.

**Adjacent fields, never identical ones.** Close enough to understand each other, far enough apart to ask the naive questions that hurt.

**A fixed date.** "Let's do this again soon" never happens. A recurring slot in a calendar does.

**A mandatory round of updates.** Everyone says where they are, including — especially — when there's nothing impressive to report. The empty months are when the group earns its keep.

## FAQ {#faq}

<div class="ds-accordion ds-accordion--flush">

<details class="ds-accordion__item">
<summary class="ds-accordion__trigger"><span>How is this different from a paid mastermind?</span><span class="ds-accordion__icon" aria-hidden="true"></span></summary>
<div class="ds-accordion__panel" markdown="1">
Price changes the dynamic. When someone pays, they expect a deliverable and the facilitator is incentivised to satisfy them. Between peers with no transaction, nobody has to spare anybody. A paid mastermind can be excellent — it's just a different thing, and it doesn't replace this.
</div>
</details>

<details class="ds-accordion__item">
<summary class="ds-accordion__trigger"><span>What if there's nobody around you?</span><span class="ds-accordion__icon" aria-hidden="true"></span></summary>
<div class="ds-accordion__panel" markdown="1">
Local studio associations and industry events exist precisely for this — that's where I met most of the people I work with now. The classic mistake is going to find clients; go to find two people you can talk to every month.
</div>
</details>

<details class="ds-accordion__item">
<summary class="ds-accordion__trigger"><span>How much time does it cost?</span><span class="ds-accordion__icon" aria-hidden="true"></span></summary>
<div class="ds-accordion__panel" markdown="1">
A few hours a month, plus the longer formats when they're warranted. That's real, and it's time not going into production. It's also the only time that has stopped me spending entire quarters heading in the wrong direction, which makes the trade fairly easy.
</div>
</details>

<details class="ds-accordion__item">
<summary class="ds-accordion__trigger"><span>Do you share actual numbers?</span><span class="ds-accordion__icon" aria-hidden="true"></span></summary>
<div class="ds-accordion__panel" markdown="1">
As far as the group can handle. The useful conversations generally start the moment someone gives a real figure instead of an impression — because that's when you find out you'd been comparing yourself to an imaginary average.
</div>
</details>
