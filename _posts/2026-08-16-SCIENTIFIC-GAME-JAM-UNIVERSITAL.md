A **Scientific Game Jam** works like this: researchers pitch their actual work, teams of developers pick one, and 48 hours later there's a game about it. The researcher stays with the team the whole time.

It is a strange brief. You're not building your idea — **you're building someone else's subject, with the expert sitting next to you.** That constraint turned out to be the most instructive thing about the weekend.

Our game was called **Universital**.

## The brief nobody warns you about {#the-brief}

In a normal jam the theme is a word and the interpretation is yours. Here the theme is a **body of real work**, owned by a person who will be in the room when you get it wrong.

That flips the first hours completely. You don't brainstorm — **you interview.** The design question stops being "what could this be" and becomes "what is the actual tension in this work, and can a player feel it?"

It's uncomfortable at first, and then it's a relief. Most jam teams lose Saturday morning to deciding what the game is about. We couldn't, because the subject already existed.

## What Universital actually is {#the-game}

*Universital* is a cooperative game set in a hospital. Players have to keep the clinical side running — the patients, the duty, the things that cannot wait — **while also making progress on academic research**, which can always wait and therefore never happens.

On top of that sit two resources you have to manage: **stress and motivation.**

That's the whole game. It reads as a resource-management loop, and it is one. It also describes a real working life closely enough that people who live it recognised it immediately.

## The best mechanic came from the researcher {#the-mechanic}

Here's the part worth taking away.

We didn't invent the central tension. We asked what the hard part of the job was, and the answer was: **the research and the clinical work compete for the same person, and only one of them has a deadline attached.**

That sentence is a game mechanic. Two demands, one pool of time, asymmetric urgency. You barely have to design it — you have to *notice* it.

I've come to think that's the general lesson of these jams. **When you're adapting real work, the mechanic is usually already there, described as a frustration.** People tell you their constraints without knowing they're handing you a design. Your job is to translate rather than to invent.

The stress-and-motivation resources came from the same conversation. They aren't a game-y abstraction bolted on for difficulty; they're the thing that actually determines whether the work happens.

## Why it had to be cooperative {#coop}

Competitive would have been easier to build and completely wrong.

The subject isn't people beating each other. It's a group of people absorbing a load that doesn't shrink, where one person going under makes everyone else's job harder. **Cooperation isn't a mode here, it's the argument.**

That's a small decision with big consequences over 48 hours — co-op means shared state, which means more to get working and less time to polish it. We took the hit because the alternative would have communicated something false about the subject.

---

## What 48 hours forces you to cut {#48h}

Everything that isn't the tension.

No progression systems. No content variety. No tutorial worth the name. What survives a two-day build is **the loop and whatever makes the loop legible** — and on a subject-driven jam, legibility is the deliverable. Nobody is going to play this for forty hours; they're going to play it for six minutes and either understand the researcher's world or not.

That reframing helps enormously under time pressure. **You aren't shipping a game, you're shipping an explanation people can operate.**

It's the same lesson our Global Game Jam weekend taught, from a different direction — that one is written up in the [Furotako jam vlog post]({{ '/2025/03/03/VLOG-GGJ2025-FUROTAKO/' | relative_url }}).

## What the public reaction told us {#reception}

The game went down well with the public, and the interesting part was *who* reacted hardest.

People with no connection to hospitals played it as a slightly frantic co-op game. People who work in that world played the same six minutes and laughed in a very specific way — the laugh of recognition, not amusement.

**That's the metric for this format.** Not "was it fun", but "did someone whose life this is say *yes, that's it*". If your mechanic is faithful, that reaction is available. If you invented the tension yourself, it isn't.

## If you're doing one of these {#advice}

Four things I'd do again:

**Spend the first hours interviewing, not designing.** The design is inside the interview.

**Look for the sentence with a conflict in it.** "I have to do X but Y always comes first" is a game. "My research is about Z" is a topic.

**Keep the researcher in the loop through Saturday night.** They will catch a distortion you can't see, and late is much cheaper than after.

**Decide what the six minutes must communicate, and cut against that.** Every jam runs out of time; the teams that land are the ones that knew in advance what they'd sacrifice.

## FAQ {#faq}

<summary class="ds-accordion__trigger"><span>Do you need a scientific background to join one?</span><span class="ds-accordion__icon" aria-hidden="true"></span></summary>
<div class="ds-accordion__panel" markdown="1">
No — and being an outsider helps. The researcher supplies the expertise; you supply the ability to ask why something matters without embarrassment. The naive question is the useful one, because it's also the player's question.
</div>
</details>

<details class="ds-accordion__item">
<summary class="ds-accordion__trigger"><span>Isn't the result just an educational game?</span><span class="ds-accordion__icon" aria-hidden="true"></span></summary>
<div class="ds-accordion__panel" markdown="1">
Only if you design it as a quiz. The distinction is whether the player learns by being told or by being placed under the same constraint. A game where you experience an impossible workload teaches something a slide about workload cannot, and it stays a game while doing it.
</div>
</details>

<details class="ds-accordion__item">
<summary class="ds-accordion__trigger"><span>What's the hardest part of the 48 hours?</span><span class="ds-accordion__icon" aria-hidden="true"></span></summary>
<div class="ds-accordion__panel" markdown="1">
Resisting the second idea. Around hour twenty someone always has a better concept, and it usually is better — and adopting it guarantees you finish nothing. Write it down, keep building the first one.
</div>
